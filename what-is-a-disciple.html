What Does It Really Mean to Be a Disciple of Jesus?

It is possible to participate in Christianity without ever seriously asking what Jesus expected of the people who followed Him. We may begin attending church, be baptized, change what we listen to, clean up our language, and leave behind habits we know do not belong in the life of a Christian. Scripture does call us to these kinds of changes. Paul tells believers to put away “anger, wrath, malice, slander, and obscene talk” (Colossians 3:8). But even these changes, important as they are, do not by themselves answer the deeper question Jesus puts before us.

Am I actually following Jesus?

FOLLOW

Jesus Called People to Follow Him.

Before they were ever called Christians, the followers of Jesus were known simply as His disciples. Acts tells us that “the disciples were first called Christians in Antioch” (Acts 11:26). The word Christian appears only a few times in the New Testament, while disciple and disciples appear again and again. The earliest followers of Jesus were known as people who had answered His call to follow Him.

What Does the Name Mean Now?

Today, however, the word Christian can mean many different things. It can describe a personal faith, a family tradition, a church affiliation, a cultural identity, or simply the answer someone gives when asked what religion they belong to. Because the word has become so broad, it is possible to call ourselves Christians without ever seriously asking the question that the original name assumed: Am I actually a disciple of Jesus?

In the Gospels, Jesus did not merely invite people to adopt a religious identity. He called them to come after Him, learn from Him, remain in His word, obey Him, and follow Him.

The issue, then, is not whether the word Christian is the right word. The question is whether the name still carries its original meaning: someone who belongs to Christ and follows Him.

“If anyone would come after me, let him deny himself and take up his cross and follow me.”Matthew 16:24

Jesus did not call people merely to identify with Him. He called them to follow Him.

A disciple begins by listening, learning, and staying near the Teacher.

COST

Jesus Did Not Hide the Cost.

Luke 14 records large crowds traveling with Jesus. Rather than making His invitation easier, Jesus turned and told them plainly what following Him meant. He spoke about competing loyalties, carrying the cross, and surrendering every competing claim upon their lives.

“So therefore, any one of you who does not renounce all that he has cannot be my disciple.”Luke 14:33

Jesus was not describing an optional level of devotion for a few unusually committed believers. He was telling the crowd what it meant to become His disciple. Family, possessions, ambitions, security, and even our own lives cannot hold a higher claim on us than He does.

This does not mean that every disciple must abandon family, possessions, work, or ordinary responsibilities. It means they are no longer ours to place above Christ. Jesus has become Lord, and everything we are and everything we have now comes under His authority.

My life is no longer my own.

CROSS

They Knew What a Cross Meant.

For us, the cross is a familiar symbol of Christianity. We see it on churches, jewelry, artwork, and Bibles. But when Jesus told His disciples to take up their cross and follow Him, they knew what a cross represented. A person carrying a cross was walking toward death.

But Jesus would soon give that image a meaning His disciples could not yet fully understand. He would carry His own cross. He did not give His life merely because Rome had the power to take it from Him. Jesus said, “No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord” (John 10:18). He could have called upon His Father and received “more than twelve legions of angels” (Matthew 26:53). Yet He willingly went to the cross in obedience to His Father and in love for us.

The Cross He Carried · The Cross We Carry

That changes how we hear His command to take up our cross. The cross is not merely the death of our old life. It is the surrender of our right to place ourselves first. A disciple learns the way of his Master: loving God above himself and loving his neighbor even when that love becomes costly.

Jesus described that kind of love plainly: “Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends” (John 15:13). For most disciples, taking up the cross will be lived out through countless acts of daily self-denial, obedience, service, forgiveness, sacrifice, and love. But the cross leaves no artificial stopping point. If faithfulness to Christ should ultimately cost us our lives, the disciple has already settled the question of who comes first.

Taking up our cross therefore means more than leaving an old life behind. It means learning the self-giving love of Christ, a love willing to bear the cost of obedience all the way to death.

“By this we know love, because He laid down His life for us. And we also ought to lay down our lives for the brethren.”1 John 3:16, NKJV

The cross marks the end of living for ourselves. Christ laid down His life for us, and He calls us to learn that same self-giving love toward God and toward one another.

IDENTITY

Church Attendance Is Not the Same as Discipleship.

Jesus warned that calling Him Lord is not the same as submitting to Him as Lord.

“Why do you call me ‘Lord, Lord,’ and not do what I tell you?”Luke 6:46

That question should make anyone who bears the name Christian to stop and think. Going to church, being baptized, serving in ministry, and identifying with Christianity can all be part of genuine faith. But none of them, by themselves, answer Jesus' question. If He is Lord, am I learning to do what He says?

Jesus' warning goes even further. He described people who would call Him “Lord” and claim to have done remarkable things in His name. Yet they would still discover that outward identification with Him was not the same as actually belonging to Him. A disciple may still fall into sin, but he does not make peace with it. He repents of that sin, turns back to Christ, and continues following Him.

That warning is especially sobering because outwardly impressive ministry is not necessarily evidence that a person belongs to Christ. Someone may be a gifted preacher, draw large crowds, speak powerfully about Jesus, or even point to remarkable works done in His name, while his life bears little resemblance to the Lord he professes to serve. Jesus tells us not to be impressed by the appearance alone.

“Not everyone who says to Me, ‘Lord, Lord,’ shall enter the kingdom of heaven... Many will say to Me in that day, ‘Lord, Lord, have we not prophesied in Your name, cast out demons in Your name, and done many wonders in Your name?’”Matthew 7:21–22, NKJV
“And then I will declare to them, ‘I never knew you; depart from Me, you who practice lawlessness!’”Matthew 7:23, NKJV

That should make us stop. They called Him Lord. They spoke and acted in His name. They pointed to extraordinary works. Yet Jesus exposed something more important than their religious résumé: “I never knew you.”

This is why discipleship cannot be settled merely by asking what Christian activities we have participated in or what we have accomplished in Christ's name. The deeper question remains: Do I actually belong to Him, know Him, and live under His authority?

Jesus described His disciples as people who abide in His word, love one another, bear fruit, deny themselves, take up their cross, and follow Him.

Outward Association

It is possible to identify with Christ while still living as though our life belongs to us.

I may call myself a Christian while still holding tightly to my own dreams, desires, possessions, and even the people I love most. The question is whether Christ truly comes before them all.

A Disciple

A disciple does more than identify with Christ. A disciple follows Him.

The evidence is not sinless perfection, but a life increasingly shaped by Jesus: remaining in His word, obeying what He taught, loving as He loved, bearing fruit, and when he sins, repenting, turning back to Christ, and continuing to follow Him.

Are we making disciples of Christ, or have we settled for filling the pews on Sunday?

DISCIPLESHIP

A Disciple Is Not a “Super Christian” With Gifts or Abilities Unavailable to Ordinary Believers.

It is easy to imagine two callings: ordinary Christians who believe in Jesus, and disciples who choose a more serious life of obedience. But the New Testament does not give us a comfortable category for belonging to Christ while continuing to live for our own ambitions, attitudes, priorities, and desires. To follow Jesus is to surrender our claim to them. We are learning to take up His ambitions, His attitudes, His priorities, and to hold dear what He holds dear. His ways become our ways because we now belong to Him.

This is what it means to live in His name. We are no longer building a life around ourselves and asking Jesus to bless it. We have been adopted into God’s family, and we are learning to live as children of our Father.

“And He died for all, that those who live should live no longer for themselves, but for Him who died for them and rose again.”2 Corinthians 5:15, NKJV

This matters because it is possible to place our confidence in a past prayer, baptism, church membership, or profession of faith while continuing to live largely for ourselves. But when Christ becomes our Lord, we have changed allegiances. Sin may still tempt us and we may still stumble, but we are no longer called to obey it, defend it, or arrange our lives around it.

“Little children, let no one deceive you. Whoever practices righteousness is righteous, as he is righteous.”1 John 3:7

John begins with a warning: “let no one deceive you.” He has already made clear that Christians can still sin. The difference is not that a disciple never falls, but that sin is no longer something to protect, excuse, or deliberately continue in as though Christ's sacrifice makes obedience unnecessary. When a disciple sins, he confesses it, turns from it, and returns to Christ.

The question is not whether I once called myself a Christian. The question is whether I belong to Christ and am following Him.

ALLEGIANCE

Whose Mark Does Your Life Bear?

Scripture presents humanity as belonging to a master. We can be slaves to sin, or through Christ become servants of righteousness and servants of God. The question is not whether we serve a master, but which master we serve.

“You who were once slaves of sin have become obedient from the heart...”Romans 6:17

Paul's language is about ownership and obedience. Romans 6 goes on to describe believers as having been set free from sin and having become servants of God. Revelation takes this question of belonging and presents it through vivid pictures of seals, names, foreheads, hands, worship, and allegiance.

God's Seal
His servants bear His name.

Revelation 7:3 describes the servants of God as sealed on their foreheads. Revelation 14:1 shows the Lamb's people with His name and His Father's name written on their foreheads. The image identifies a people who belong to God.

“...seal the servants of our God on their foreheads.”Revelation 7:3
The Beast's Mark
Its followers bear another mark.

Revelation 13 places the beast's mark on the right hand or forehead. Revelation 14:9 directly joins receiving that mark with worshiping the beast and its image. The imagery is therefore tied to allegiance, worship, and whom a person serves.

“...receives a mark on his forehead or on his hand...”Revelation 14:9

The imagery also has deep roots in the Old Testament. Israel was told to keep God's words in their hearts and to bind them as a sign on their hands and as frontlets between their eyes. These outward signs pointed to something deeper: God's truth was to govern both how His people thought and how they lived. Under the New Covenant, God promised to put His law within His people and write it on their hearts (Jeremiah 31:33; Hebrews 8:10). Jesus spoke of this same movement beyond outward forms when He told the woman at the well that true worship would not be defined by a mountain or Jerusalem, but that true worshipers would worship the Father “in spirit and truth” (John 4:21–24).

God's truth was no longer merely to be placed before their eyes and upon their hands—it was to be written upon their hearts and minds and lived out through their lives.

“You shall bind them as a sign on your hand, and they shall be as frontlets between your eyes.”Deuteronomy 6:8

There is also an ancient historical background that helps us hear the force of being visibly marked. In the Greco-Roman world, slaves and criminals could be tattooed or branded, and runaway slaves in particular could be marked on the face or forehead as a punishment and means of identification. That history should not be made to carry the interpretation of Revelation, but it helps us understand how powerful the language of a visible mark of belonging would have sounded in that world.

Scripture's larger picture is consistent: God has servants who bear His name, the beast has worshipers who bear its mark, and Paul speaks plainly of masters, obedience, slavery, and freedom.

The deeper question is ownership. Whose thoughts are becoming my thoughts, and whose will is being carried out through what I do?

Forehead · Thoughts
Am I learning to think like Christ?

What do I believe? What occupies my thoughts? Whose values are becoming mine? A disciple learns to see the world increasingly through the teaching and character of Christ.

Hand · Actions
Am I obediently carrying those thoughts into my actions?

What we believe should eventually become visible in what we do. A disciple does not merely learn the thoughts and ways of Christ; he learns to obey them. His faith reaches his hands through his service, generosity, mercy, honesty, relationships, and the good works God created him to do.

A disciple should increasingly bear the family resemblance of Christ.

Remain

“If you abide in my word, you are truly my disciples.” John 8:31

Love

“By this all people will know that you are my disciples, if you have love for one another.” John 13:35

Bear Fruit

“By this my Father is glorified, that you bear much fruit and so prove to be my disciples.” John 15:8

The evidence is not perfection. Disciples still stumble, but they repent, return to Christ, and keep following. Over time, the direction of the life begins to reveal whose we are.

“Turn away from evil and do good; seek peace and pursue it. The eyes of the LORD are toward the righteous and his ears toward their cry.”Psalm 34:14–15

You should not need a label for your life to eventually give away whom you follow.

GROWTH

New Life Begins Before Maturity Arrives.

A new Christian does not immediately become like Christ, even though that is the goal. God has begun His work in us, but disciples need time to learn Scripture, recognize sin, practice obedience, receive correction, develop wisdom, and grow in love.

New believers were never meant simply to be baptized and left to figure everything out alone. Mature Christians should walk beside them, teach them, model faithfulness, answer questions, and help them learn the way of Jesus.

“Go therefore and make disciples of all nations... teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you.”Matthew 28:19–20

Jesus made disciples, then commissioned disciples to make more disciples.

At some point, what we have learned has to become the road we actually walk.

MATURITY

New Faith Should Burn Brightly, but Maturity Takes Time.

A new believer does not need years of study before telling someone about Christ. After the woman at the well encountered Jesus, she immediately returned to her town and invited people to come see Him. Her understanding was still developing, but she could tell people about the One she had encountered.

“Come, see a man who told me all that I ever did. Can this be the Christ?”John 4:29

That excitement should be encouraged, not suppressed. A new believer can tell family, friends, and coworkers what Christ has done and invite them to discover Him too.

But sharing a testimony is different from being ready to teach, lead, or carry responsibility for others. The New Testament places real weight on proven character, faithfulness, sound teaching, and maturity when spiritual responsibility is involved. Enthusiasm is valuable, but enthusiasm and maturity are not the same thing.

The first disciples themselves stumbled, misunderstood Jesus, and needed correction. But Jesus formed them before entrusting them with greater responsibility. In the same way, a young disciple needs strengthening and protection while roots grow deep.

“He must not be a recent convert, or he may become puffed up with conceit...”1 Timothy 3:6

Paul is speaking specifically about an overseer, but the principle is clear: a new believer should be given time to grow before being entrusted with spiritual leadership.

New believers need room to burn brightly without being asked to carry weight they are not ready to carry.

Maturity begins to bear fruit when a disciple can help another person walk the road.

FRUIT

A Disciple's Life Should Bear Fruit.

Jesus did not describe fruit as an optional extra for especially mature Christians. In John 15, fruit is part of what happens when a disciple remains in Him. The branch does not produce fruit by separating itself from the vine and trying harder. It bears fruit because it remains connected to the source of its life.

“Abide in Me, and I in you. As the branch cannot bear fruit of itself, unless it abides in the vine, neither can you, unless you abide in Me.”John 15:4, NKJV

Fruit is not the price of belonging to Christ. It is what begins to grow from a life that remains in Christ.

The Fruit of the Spirit

What the Spirit is forming in us.

Paul describes the fruit of the Spirit as love, joy, peace, longsuffering, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control. This is the character of the disciple being changed from within.

“But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control.”Galatians 5:22–23, NKJV
The Fruit of a Disciple

What a life following Christ begins to produce.

As Christ shapes our character, that inward work becomes visible through obedience, love, service, generosity, good works, witness, and eventually helping others follow Him. The fruit of the disciple is a life that increasingly glorifies the Father.

“By this My Father is glorified, that you bear much fruit; so you will be My disciples.”John 15:8, NKJV

These are not two unrelated kinds of fruit. The Spirit changes who we are, and that changed character begins to shape what we do. Love becomes service. Generosity becomes giving. Compassion becomes mercy. Faithfulness becomes obedience. A disciple's fruit grows outward from the life Christ is producing within.

The fruit does not create the disciple. The fruit reveals the life of the Vine flowing through the disciple.

THE PATH

Come. Follow. Grow. Help Others Follow.

There is no mechanical timetable for maturity. People grow at different rates, and every disciple remains dependent on God's grace. But the New Testament gives us a recognizable direction.

Come to Christ→Learn to Follow Christ→Grow in Christ→Help Others Follow Christ

This is not a ladder by which we earn heaven. It is the life of a disciple: receiving Christ, walking with Him, being changed by Him, and eventually becoming someone who can faithfully help another person walk the same road.

WEDDING

The Road Leads to the Marriage Supper of the Lamb.

Jesus often spoke of God's kingdom with the picture of a banquet or wedding feast. In His parables, people who were invited found reasons not to come. Some were too occupied with possessions, work, or ordinary life to recognize the importance of the invitation. The warning is sobering because an invitation can be heard and still refused.

Revelation brings that wedding picture to its great conclusion. The Bride has made herself ready, and the saints are pictured in fine linen. The final picture is not of people who merely adopted a Christian name. It is a people who belong to the Lamb.

“Let us be glad and rejoice, and give honour to him: for the marriage of the Lamb is come, and his wife hath made herself ready. And to her was granted that she should be arrayed in fine linen, clean and white: for the fine linen is the righteousness of saints.”Revelation 19:7–8, KJV

The fine linen does not mean the saints purchased their place at the table by righteous works. The invitation, the Lamb, forgiveness, new life, and even the Bride's preparation all begin with God's grace. But grace does not make the life that follows meaningless. Revelation does not picture a Bride indifferent to the Bridegroom. She has made herself ready.

That is why counting the cost is not a gloomy addition to the Gospel. Jesus is worth the cost. He is not merely calling us away from an old life. He is calling us to Himself.

Do not make excuses when the invitation comes. Follow the One who invited you.

THE QUESTION TO TAKE WITH YOU

Am I Actually Following Jesus?

Not simply: Do I call myself a Christian? Do I attend church? Have I completed the expected religious steps? The deeper question is whether Jesus is truly my Lord and Teacher, and whether His way is increasingly becoming visible in the way I think, love, speak, choose, and live.
